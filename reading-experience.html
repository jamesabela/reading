Optical Reading Simulator

An educational tool for exploring reported visual experiences.

Important: This is a simulation of some reported visual experiences. It is for awareness and discussion only. It is not a diagnostic tool. Experiences vary greatly from person to person.

Simulation Presets

Optical Effects

Some students report that letters appear to vibrate or shake on the page, making it hard to fixate on a single word.

Words may seem to fade in and out or change in brightness, creating a flickering effect that leads to eye strain.

Readers often feel that lines of text are sliding out of alignment or floating away from each other.

Vision can periodically become "fuzzy" or out of focus, as if the eyes are struggling to maintain a sharp image.

Letters and lines may appear to bunch together or overlap, making it difficult to distinguish individual characters.

It can be challenging to move from the end of one word to the beginning of the next without losing the place.

Text Challenges

Mirror-image letters like 'b' and 'd' or 'p' and 'q' are frequently swapped or misread.

The internal letters of a word can appear to switch places, making familiar words look unrecognizable.

Students may frequently confuse common words that look visually similar, such as "was" and "saw."

Short function words like "the" or "of" are often overlooked during rapid scanning of a sentence.

Global Intensity

Adjusting Experience...
The student sat at their desk, looking at the page filled with black text. At first, the words seemed steady and clear, but as they focused on the central paragraph, a strange feeling began to take hold. It wasn't that they couldn't read the words, but rather that the paper itself felt less stable than it should be. The bright white background seemed to glare, making it difficult to keep their place as they moved from one line to the next. They tried to concentrate on a single sentence about the ancient history of Rome, but the letters felt like they were vibrating slightly. By the time they reached the middle of the page, the effort of keeping the letters still was already making their eyes feel heavy and tired.

Things that can help

Standard "Newspaper" fonts like Times New Roman are not recommended for accessible reading. Instead, specialized fonts like Lexend and OpenDyslexic reduce visual clutter and improve focus.

Increasing text size reduces the precision required for eye tracking and allows users to process word shapes more easily.

Increased line spacing creates a clear visual gap between rows, preventing the eye from accidentally skipping to the wrong line.

A global tint filters specific light wavelengths, reducing the "glare" that causes text to shimmer or move for many with visual stress.

Breaking long text into shorter segments or summaries helps reduce cognitive load and makes content easier to process.

Left alignment prevents "rivers of white" which can be visually confusing when text is spaced unevenly.

First-line indents create a clear visual anchor, helping the eyes return to the start of the correct line after a break.

A reading ruler isolates a single line, preventing vertical "eye jumping" and helping users maintain their place.

Low contrast themes (like cream and grey) can significantly reduce the eye strain caused by bright screen glare.
